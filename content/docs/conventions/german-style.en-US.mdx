German is the longest locale on the site, and its readers are German-speaking engineers and buyers who notice a sentence translated word for word at once. Every German message in `messages/de-DE/`, docs page, blog post and changelog entry follows this page. The glossary at the end is the single source of the German terms: `GERMAN_TERMS`, `GERMAN_NAMES` and `GERMAN_PHRASES` in `src/data/german-glossary.ts`, which this page prints. A term that the glossary does not decide yet is added there first, in the same pull request that uses it.

## Write the meaning, not the sentence

Read the English, close it and write what a German senior engineer would write to say the same thing to a colleague or a customer. Keep the facts, the numbers, the placeholders and the tags; change everything else that makes the sentence sound translated.

- German puts the verb to work where English stacks nouns: `Die Projektgenerierung kommt mit einer späteren Version`, not `Die Funktionalität der Projektgenerierung wird ausgeliefert`.
- False friends change the meaning: `kontrollieren` means to inspect, not to control; `eventuell` means possibly, not eventually; `realisieren` means to carry out, not to notice; a `Schnittstelle` is an interface between systems, while a user interface is the `Oberfläche`; `Quelle` is where a claim comes from, while code is `Quellcode`. A software engineer is an `Entwickler`, not an `Ingenieur`.
- An English punchline gets a German sentence that makes the same point, not the same words: `You just shouldn’t have to` is `Du solltest es nur nicht noch einmal bauen müssen`, not `Du solltest es einfach nicht müssen`.
- German words are the longest of the nine locales, so a slogan, a heading and a button lose words rather than meaning, and a change to the landing page or the pricing is checked for its line breaks at desktop and phone width.

## Form of address and register

The site says du to every reader, in lowercase as Duden recommends: `du`, `dein`, `dich`, `dir`. The account pages, the emails and the legal pages do too, because the form of address has no legal effect. [One form of address](/docs/conventions/translations#one-form-of-address) explains the check that fails `Sie`, `Ihr`, `Ihnen` and a capitalised `Du` or `Dein` inside a sentence.

- Buttons, links and menu entries are infinitives (`Core kaufen`, `Befehl kopieren`, `Preise ansehen`); headings and table headers are nouns (`Einstellungen`, `Lizenz`, `Bestellungen`); confirmations are participles (`Kopiert`, `Gespeichert`, `Aktivierungsplatz freigegeben`).
- Running text addresses the reader with the imperative of du: `Kopiere den Befehl, sobald er zu deiner Architektur passt.`
- An error says what failed and what to do next, without an apology: `Die Kurse konnten nicht geladen werden. Du findest sie alle auf der Kursseite.`
- The tone is direct, confident and precise. No exclamation marks, no superlatives the facts do not carry, and no sales phrases that only exist in English marketing.

## Compounds and hyphens

German writes a compound as one word, and a word it has taken from English joins it like any other: `Kundenbereich`, `Lizenzschlüssel`, `Adminbereich`, `Landingpage`, `Starterkit`, `Teamplatz`.

- A hyphen joins the parts when one of them is an abbreviation, a digit, a name or a code identifier, or when the joined word would be hard to read: `E-Mail-Adresse`, `CI-Lauf`, `D1-Datenbank`, `Polar-Checkout`, `Webhook-Ereignis`.
- An English compound of two nouns takes a hyphen, never a space: `Pull-Request`, `Merge-Queue`, `Rate-Limit`, `Code-Review`, `Use-Case`, `Release-Notes`. An English adjective and its noun may stay apart, as Duden allows for `Open Source`: `Breaking Change`, `Magic Link`.
- When a compound contains a term of several words, a hyphen joins every part (Durchkopplung): `Pull-Request-Beschreibung`, `TanStack-Start-Serverfunktionen`, `SaaSy-Land-Codebasis`, `End-to-End-Test`. Never `Pull Request-Beschreibung` or `TanStack Start Serverfunktionen`.
- A product name keeps the spelling its owner gives it when it stands alone: `Durable Objects`, `Server Components`, `Content Security Policy`, `React Email`.

## English terms of art

German engineers use some English terms as German nouns, and the site keeps them: `Webhook`, `Deployment`, `Worker`, `Build`, `Commit`, `Branch`, `Merge`, `Release`, `Stack`, `Framework`, `Repository`, `Checkout`, `Dashboard`, `Badge`, `Passkey` and `Pull-Request`. They are written without italics or quotation marks, take a German article and a capital, and form German verbs and participles: `der Build`, `das Deployment`, `das Release`, `die Repositorys`, `deployen`, `deployt`, `committet`, `gemergt`, never `deployed` or `gemerged`. `die CLI` is feminine, after `die Schnittstelle`.

Everything that has a standard German word is translated: a `tier` is a `Paket`, a `seat` a `Platz`, a `refund` an `Erstattung`, a `tenant` a `Mandant`, a `server function` a `Serverfunktion` and `AI` is `KI`. Two words need care because German already uses them for something else:

- `Bereitstellung` is the consumer-law term for supplying digital content, which starts the clock on the right of withdrawal, so a deploy is a `Deployment`, never a `Bereitstellung`.
- A `Rate` in German is an instalment, so the mechanism is `Rate-Limiting` and the limit a `Rate-Limit`, never `Ratenbegrenzung`.

## Gendered language

The reader is always du, so the copy never needs a gendered noun for them. For roles in running text the site uses the generic masculine (`der Käufer`, `Entwickler`, `Nutzer`, `der Gründer`) and no gender star, colon or capital I, so never `Nutzer*innen`, `Nutzer:innen` or `NutzerInnen`. Where an admin page or a legal text describes one buyer whose record it shows, it names both forms: `die Käuferin oder der Käufer`. A noun that is neutral already is welcome: `Lernende`.

## Typography

- Quotation marks are „…“, with ‚…‘ inside them. Code and identifiers keep their own quotes inside inline code.
- A dash between clauses is an en dash with a space on each side (`–`), never a hyphen. A range takes an en dash without spaces: `10–20`.
- After a colon, a complete sentence starts with a capital letter (`Er darf so schnell schreiben, wie er will: Fehlerhafter Code gelangt nicht nach main.`); a word, a list or a fragment stays lowercase (`Ja, für den Anwendungscode: Anweisungen, Verzweigungen, Funktionen und Zeilen`).
- Headings, titles, buttons and menu entries follow German capitalisation, which capitalises nouns and nothing else: `Das stabile Fundament`, not `Das Stabile Fundament`.
- German needs a comma before every subordinate clause (`dass`, `weil`, `wenn`, `sobald`, a relative pronoun) and before an infinitive group with `um`, `ohne` or `statt`, and takes no comma before `und` or `oder` in a list.
- Numbers, dates, currencies and units come from the formatter, which writes a period between thousands (`50.000`), a decimal comma (`0,50 $`), a space before the percent and currency signs (`100 %`) and dates as `17. November 2026`, so they are never typed. A value the code defines is printed with `<RepositoryFact>`.

## The glossary check [#glossary-check]

`bun run check` runs `check:glossary`, which fails whenever German copy uses a term from a never column below: the messages in `messages/de-DE/`, the emails among them, every German docs page, blog post and changelog entry with its title, description and questions, the navigation titles in `meta.de-DE.json` and the German text of `content/courses/saasy-land-courses.json`. It prints the file, the line, the word it found and the term the glossary prefers. The same check reads the Polish copy against the [Polish glossary](/docs/conventions/polish-style#glossary-check).

- A term fails in each of its inflected forms and as the first part of a compound. In `src/data/german-glossary.ts` a bar marks where the ending of a word starts, so `Stufe|` fails `Stufen` and `Stufenkarte` but not `Graustufen`; the tables below print the term without the bar. A word without a bar fails only as written, so `Workspace` does not fail `Bun-Workspace` or `Workspaces`.
- Code, links, paths, quotations in „…“ and ‚…‘ and an English term given in parentheses after `engl.` are not German prose, so the check skips them.
- A capitalised term fails wherever it appears, which covers German nouns; a lowercase term also fails where a sentence, a heading, a list item, a table cell or a link text starts.
- Another locale joins the check with a glossary of its own in `src/data/` and an entry in `GLOSSARIES` in `src/lib/glossary-terms.ts` that names its locale, its quotation marks and its abbreviation for English.
