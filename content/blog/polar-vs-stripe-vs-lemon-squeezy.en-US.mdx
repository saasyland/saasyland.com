## The question that decides most of it: who sells your software?

Every sale has a seller: the business your customer contracts with, which owes the VAT or sales tax on the sale, issues the invoice and answers for refunds and chargebacks. Payments people call it the **merchant of record**.

**A payment processor moves the money and leaves you as the seller.** Stripe's regular products work this way. Stripe's own comparison table puts it in one cell: with its other products, the merchant of record is "your business". Stripe Tax can calculate and collect the tax for you, but the tax is still owed by you.

**A merchant of record resells your product.** Polar describes itself as "a reseller of your digital goods & services", and its buyer terms say that the buyer purchases the product from Polar while you license the right to use it. Lemon Squeezy works the same way, and so does Stripe Managed Payments, Stripe's own merchant of record, where the buyer sees the purchase as sold through Link.

The rest of this article follows from that one line on the receipt. If you are the seller, the obligations of a seller in every country you sell to are yours to meet. If a merchant of record is the seller, they are mostly its obligations, and its fee pays for that.

## What a merchant of record does that a processor does not

### VAT and sales tax

Selling software to consumers abroad creates tax obligations in their countries, not only in yours. In the EU, telecommunications, broadcasting and electronic services sold to consumers are taxed in the customer's country, at that country's rate. An EU business whose cross-border sales of this kind stay under EUR 10,000 a year may charge its own country's VAT instead, and the One-Stop Shop lets a seller register in one EU country to declare and pay the VAT due everywhere else. Sellers outside the EU have a non-Union version of the same scheme. In the United States, the Supreme Court ruled in _South Dakota v. Wayfair_ (2018) that a state may require a seller with no physical presence there to collect its sales tax, and the rules differ from state to state.

With a processor, every one of those registrations and returns is yours. Stripe Tax helps a great deal: it calculates the tax for each transaction, tracks your sales against registration thresholds and helps you register and file, directly or through partners. It does not change who owes the tax.

With a merchant of record, the tax on each sale belongs to the seller, which is the merchant of record. Polar says it takes on "the liability of international sales taxes globally" and handles "filings and remittance on an ongoing basis". You still owe income tax on what it pays you, in your own country, as Polar's page also points out.

### Invoices, refunds and chargebacks

**Polar.** Customers download receipts and invoices in Polar's customer portal and can add a company name, a VAT number or a billing address to an invoice themselves. You refund an order in full or in part from the dashboard; the tax is refunded with it, the payment fees are not. For a one-time purchase, the refund also revokes the order's benefits, licence keys included, unless you untick that option. Polar may also refund an order within 60 days of purchase at its own discretion to prevent chargebacks, and a dispute costs $15 whatever its outcome.

**Stripe.** You are the seller, so the invoice, the refund and the dispute are yours to handle with Stripe's tools. Stripe charges no fee to issue most refunds, but it does not return the original processing fee, and a dispute received costs a fee that depends on your country: €20 on an Irish account, for example.

**Stripe Managed Payments.** Stripe sends receipts, invoices and refund notices to the customer from Link, handles disputes and submits the evidence itself. Customers can ask Link support for a refund, Stripe can refund within 60 days of the transaction in certain cases, and if you do not answer a support escalation within 48 hours, Stripe may refund without your approval.

**Lemon Squeezy.** Its customer portal shows the billing history and lets customers update billing details and tax IDs.

### The customer relationship

The merchant of record's name is on the sale. With Stripe Managed Payments the customer sees Link as the merchant of record, the card statement reads `LINK.COM*` followed by your statement descriptor, and the checkout cannot run on your own domain. With Polar, the buyer accepts Polar's buyer terms for the purchase and your terms for the licence. That is the price of not running a tax department: someone else's name and terms sit between you and your customer at the moment of payment.

## Fees, from each provider's own pricing page

**Polar** has four plans. Starter is free and charges 5% + 50¢ per transaction. Pro costs $20 a month and charges 3.8% + 40¢, Growth $100 a month and 3.6% + 35¢, Scale $400 a month and 3.4% + 30¢. Cards issued outside the US add 1.5% on every plan, so a sale to a European card on Starter costs 6.5% + 50¢. Organisations created before 27 May 2026 keep the Early Member rate of 4% + 40¢, with 0.5% more for subscriptions; comparisons that quote Polar at 4% + 40¢ describe that rate, not what a new organisation pays. Payouts pass on Stripe's fees without a markup: $2 for each month with an active payout, 0.25% + $0.25 per payout, and 0.25% (in the EU) to 1% for currency conversion.

**Stripe** prices card payments by the country of your account. An Irish account pays 1.5% + €0.25 for standard EEA cards, 2.5% + €0.25 for UK cards and 3.15% + €0.25 for other international cards, plus 2% when the currency has to be converted. A Polish account pays 1.5% + 1.00 zł for standard EEA cards. On top of that, Stripe Tax's no-code plan costs 0.5% per transaction where you are registered to collect tax, Billing costs 0.7% of billing volume on pay-as-you-go, and Managed Payments costs 3.5% per successful transaction in addition to the payment fees.

**Lemon Squeezy** charges 5% + 50¢, plus 1.5% for transactions outside the US, 1.5% for PayPal and 0.5% for subscription payments. Payouts to a US bank account are free; to a bank account outside the US they cost 1% per payout.

A processor's card fee is not the cost of selling. Add the tax engine, the billing engine, the VAT and sales tax returns, and the hours you or an accountant spend on them, then compare that with a merchant of record's rate. For a small business selling to many countries, I expect that sum to favour the merchant of record; with enough volume and a finance function, it favours the processor. Polar's paid plans replace part of its per-transaction premium with a monthly fee for that reason.

## Payouts

**Polar** pays out when you ask it to. You withdraw your balance once it reaches the minimum for your currency ($10 for US dollars), Polar processes withdrawals in batches 24 hours after the request, and the money arrives in about 4 to 7 business days. Organisations created on or after 12 May 2026 wait out a 7-day settlement delay before a transaction can be paid out, and Polar may hold specific transactions longer, usually to match your refund policy.

**Stripe** pays out daily, weekly, monthly or when you ask, depending on your settings and your country. The first payout typically arrives 7 to 14 days after your first live payment, and settlement takes 7 calendar days in many European countries.

**Lemon Squeezy** processes payouts automatically twice a month, by bank transfer or PayPal.

## Licence keys, customer portal and webhooks

**Licence keys** are where the providers differ most for anyone selling software rather than access. Polar issues a unique key for each purchase as a benefit of the product, lets you cap the machines a key activates on, and exposes endpoints to activate and validate keys. Lemon Squeezy has the same idea, with an activation limit and a licence length for one-time products. Stripe has no licence keys: its Entitlements grant and revoke access to features, and a key that a CLI can activate on a machine is yours to build.

That difference decided a lot for this site. The SaaSy Land CLI activates each machine through Polar's licence keys, and Polar enforces each tier's limit: <ActivationLimit tier="core" /> machines at a time on Core, <ActivationLimit tier="complete" /> on Complete and <ActivationLimit tier="agency" /> on Agency. The [CLI licence API](/docs/features/cli-licence-api) documents the whole exchange.

**Customer portals.** Polar's portal shows orders, invoices and receipts, licence keys and other benefits, and lets customers cancel subscriptions and update their payment method. Stripe's customer portal handles billing details and tax IDs, payment methods, subscriptions and invoices, in the customer's language. Managed Payments customers manage their orders through Link. Lemon Squeezy's portal covers subscriptions, licence keys, files and billing history.

**Webhooks.** All three sign their webhooks. Polar sends events such as `order.paid`, `order.refunded`, `refund.created` and `benefit_grant.revoked`, and signing secrets created on or after 8 September 2026 follow the Standard Webhooks specification. Stripe signs each event in the `Stripe-Signature` header, retries an undelivered event for up to three days in live mode, and does not guarantee the order of events. Whichever you choose, write handlers that can run twice for the same event, because failed deliveries are retried and an event can arrive more than once. The [Payments](/docs/features/payments) page shows how saasyland.com dispatches Polar's events.

## Lemon Squeezy is closing: where to move your store

On 16 November 2026 Lemon Squeezy announced that it is winding down. New store sign-ups are closed, and it will stop processing payments on 31 March 2027, a deadline it says it cannot extend. Existing stores can keep accepting payments while they prepare. Its recommended path is Stripe Managed Payments, built by the same team, for businesses based in a supported country, with migration tooling announced for 2 December. Businesses elsewhere are asked to export their data and move to another provider.

If you sell on Lemon Squeezy today:

1. **Start with licence keys and subscriptions.** Payment history can be exported; live keys that your software validates and renewals that must not lapse are what break customers.
2. **Check eligibility before you plan around Managed Payments.** It supports businesses based in a list of countries that includes all 27 EU countries and the UK, and digital products only, sold through Stripe Checkout or Payment Links.
3. **Compare Polar if licence keys matter to you.** Of the merchants of record still taking new sellers, Polar is the one with licence keys built in.

For a new product, Lemon Squeezy is no longer an option, which turns this three-way comparison into a two-way one: a merchant of record (Polar or Stripe Managed Payments) or a processor (Stripe).

## Why saasyland.com runs on Polar

saasyland.com sells three licence tiers, Core at <TierPrice tier="core" />, Complete at <TierPrice tier="complete" /> and Agency at <TierPrice tier="agency" />, each a one-time payment, to buyers anywhere, in <RepositoryFact name="languages" /> languages. Polar fits that business for five reasons, each of which you can read in the site's source:

1. **It is the seller.** A small business in Poland does not want VAT and sales tax registrations in every market it sells to, and with Polar as the seller, the tax on those sales is Polar's.
2. **Licence keys with activation limits are built in.** The CLI's licence API activates machines through Polar, so the limits on the pricing cards are enforced by the provider, not by code we would have to keep correct.
3. **It plugs into Better Auth.** The `@polar-sh/better-auth` plugin adds the customer portal and verified webhooks to the authentication the site already runs.
4. **Checkout takes custom fields.** A required checkbox, stored on the order, is how Polar records the buyer's consent to immediate supply, which EU consumer law asks for. The [companion article](/blog/eu-right-of-withdrawal-digital-products) walks through that whole flow.
5. **The platform is open source.** Polar's code is on [GitHub](https://github.com/polarsource/polar) under the Apache 2.0 licence, so how it handles orders, refunds and disputes can be read rather than guessed.

The costs are real too. Polar's rate is higher than a processor's card fee. Payouts are withdrawals you start, with Stripe's fees passed on. Its checkout localisation is in beta and covers 15 languages, which do not include Ukrainian, one of the languages this site speaks. And Polar can refund an order within 60 days without asking, so the site's [Commercial Licence](/licence) states what any full refund does: it ends the licence for that purchase.

## When Stripe is the better choice

Choose Stripe, as a processor, when:

- **You already have the tax side.** You are registered where you need to be, file through the One-Stop Shop or have an accountant who does, and sell mostly in markets you already cover.
- **You need Stripe's lower-level tools.** A marketplace or platform built on Connect, a payment form you build yourself with Elements, or one-off invoices, none of which Managed Payments supports.
- **You have volume.** At high revenue, the difference between a card fee plus Stripe Tax and a merchant of record's rate can pay for a finance function.

Choose Stripe Managed Payments when you want Stripe's checkout and dashboard and a merchant of record, your business is in a supported country, you sell digital products only, and you can live with Stripe Checkout or Payment Links, Link's name on the receipt and no custom checkout domain.
